Adeeb Lab · Responsible Decision Intelligence

We make AI decisions auditable before they are adopted.

A research and development lab led by Prof. Adeeb Noor that works as the R&D arm for hospitals, government agencies, companies and universities. We test when an AI system's decision changes for an individual case, and build the evidence an organization needs before it adopts or updates a solution.

I study the ethics of AI-assisted decisions as a question of evidence: what changes when a system changes, who bears the consequences, and who signs off.

One vision

Our vision

No decision that affects people, whether made by a person or an AI system, is adopted without evidence that can be checked and traced, before adoption and after every change.

Our question: what changes when your AI changes?

Our mission: We build the methods, tools and evidence that make this possible, and work as the research arm for the organizations that need them.

RIDISubmission-ready

RIDI: Same score, different decision

Two versions of an AI system get the same score. Do they make the same decisions? Often not, and nobody sees it.

Problem: AI systems are judged by average scores, so a new version or an alternative system is accepted because it scores the same, even though its decisions may differ on specific cases.

The opportunity: Every organization that relies on an AI system that gets updated needs a test before accepting the update, and today it has no independent case-level test it can run on its own cases.

Pillar 1
Decision stability: It measures whether the system's decision stays the same for each case when the model changes.

The story

An illustrative sceneA hospital uses an AI assistant to answer medication questions. The vendor ships an update: 86% accuracy before, 86% after. It is approved the same day. Behind the unchanged number, one patient who would have received a correct answer now gets a wrong one, and another the opposite. Nobody knows who they are.

This question began in a PhD on drug–drug interactions, where the first lesson was that the evidence behind a decision matters as much as the decision itself. It came back in practical form during years running IT at a university hospital: how do you trust an update to a system you did not build and cannot see inside?

Today models are updated every few months or faster, and organizations buy models rather than train them. Regulatory frameworks, such as the FDA's predetermined change control plans, increasingly ask how a model change is managed after approval. What the adopting organization lacks is a test it can run from the outside, on its own cases.

The idea

Compare decisions case by case, not average to average.

  1. Two versions of the system
  2. The same cases
  3. Case-level decision comparisonour added value
  4. Report: what flipped, for whom

The opportunity

Every organization that relies on an AI system that gets updated needs a test before accepting the update, and today it has no independent case-level test it can run on its own cases.

Why now

Model updates are frequent, and regulatory frameworks increasingly ask how change is managed after approval.

What a partner gains
  • A report of what flipped, and for whom, on your own cases before you accept the update
  • A preregistered, reproducible method
  • Joint publication where the agreement allows
Who we are looking for

  • Researchers in evaluation, statistics and Arabic NLP
Ways to engage

Our five questions

1. Problem
AI systems are judged by average scores, so a new version or an alternative system is accepted because it scores the same, even though its decisions may differ on specific cases.
2. Current solution
Accuracy, AUC and leaderboards, plus techniques model developers use during training to reduce regressions after an update.
3. Why it falls short
Averages hide the cases whose decisions changed, and developer techniques need access to training, so the adopting organization has no external test.
4. Our solution
RIDI: compare the decision on every case between two versions or systems, with confidence intervals, preregistration and the open ridi-audit toolkit.
5. Impact
An organization sees what changed and for whom before accepting an update. Early result: five standard retrieval metrics stayed identical while correctness changed in 17.27% of 800 queries (not yet peer reviewed).

The journey so far

  1. DoneSubmission-ready manuscript, with an open data and reproducibility package on Zenodo
  2. DoneThe ridi-audit toolkit under MIT licence, and an OSF preregistration
  3. NowIndependent human audit of the results
  4. NextSubmission for peer review, then a preregistered confirmatory study

What we do not claim

  • A stable decision is not necessarily a correct one, and a flip is not necessarily an error.
  • The early result comes from one set of 800 queries and is not yet peer reviewed.
  • The audit is not presented as a compliance certificate.
  • The current experiment concerns retrieval and answer correctness. It does not by itself demonstrate discrimination, clinical benefit or the fairness of an institutional decision.

Pillar 3
Evidence through change: It makes the impact assessment get re-checked with evidence whenever the model changes.

The story

An illustrative sceneA compliance officer at a health organization wrote an impact assessment for an AI system before it went live, estimating the likelihood of harm on the data available then. Six months later the vendor updated the model, and the report she received says performance is "comparable". The question the report does not answer: is her assessment still valid?

This project grows directly out of RIDI. If we can measure how many decisions flipped after an update, can that measurement serve as evidence in a regulatory document? Answering that needs computing and law together, so the lab works on it with legal and compliance specialists.

Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law and its Implementing Regulation address impact assessment, and the SFDA regulates AI-based medical software. The texts set the duty, but organizations need a practical way to show their assessment still holds after each update.

The idea

Make decision flips a number an impact assessment can read.

  1. Pre-deployment impact assessment
  2. Vendor update
  3. Measure decision flips on reference casesour added value
  4. Revise the assessment or reject the update

The opportunity

Compliance officers must carry out impact assessments, but have no practical way to show an assessment still holds after a model update.

Why now

Enforcement of Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law, and the growing use of language models in healthcare.

What a partner gains
  • A practical test to attach to the impact assessment
  • Early clarity on what the evidence does and does not support
  • A say in the protocol design before it is locked
Who we are looking for
  • Compliance and data-protection officers in health organizations
  • Regulators, and model vendors who want clearer evidence with each update
Ways to engage
  • Pilot with a health organization
  • Workshop with a regulator
  • Joint research

Our five questions

1. Problem
When a vendor updates its model, an organization's impact assessment may still rest on a likelihood estimated on old data, while some cases change their decision.
2. Current solution
A pre-deployment impact assessment, aggregate performance reports from the vendor, and legal texts that set out duties.
3. Why it falls short
The texts set the duty but not how to evidence it, and aggregate reports do not show which decisions changed.
4. Our solution
Pair a legal analysis of Article 25 of the PDPL Implementing Regulation and SFDA requirements with a measurement of decision flips when a medical language model is updated.
5. Impact
A practical test compliance officers and regulators can use, with a clear statement of what the evidence does and does not support.

The journey so far

  1. DoneInitial legal analysis of the relevant texts
  2. NowWriting and locking the protocol before the full run
  3. NextRun on a medical language model, then write the paper

What we do not claim

  • It is not legal advice, and it does not replace the impact assessment itself.
  • It states what the evidence does and does not support; it does not decide compliance.

Pillar 2
Evidence traceability: It makes sure shared knowledge carries its sources and nothing that should not be disclosed.

The story

An illustrative sceneA physician has a protocol that worked in her department and wants to share it with another hospital. First she must make sure it contains no patient name or file number, that every statement in it has a source, and that the other hospital will use it on the agreed terms. Today that means weeks of review, or sharing that never happens.

This invention came out of the HEALTHx question: how does clinical knowledge become an organized asset that can be traced and shared? The work showed that the weakest link is not writing the knowledge down, but the moment it is shared.

AI summarization tools sped up writing, but added two risks: fabricating an unsupported statement, or exposing an identifier that should have been removed. The invention addresses exactly that moment. Its technical details stay confidential until filing.

The idea

A governance layer between knowledge and the moment it is shared.

  2. Governance layer (confidential until filing)our added value
  3. Sharing on agreed terms

AI summarization tools entering hospitals have raised the risk of fabrication and exposed identifiers.

Our five questions

1. Problem
Clinical and research knowledge inside hospitals is hard to share safely: patient identifiers, unsupported statements and unclear usage rights.

Pillar 2
Evidence traceability: It ties every element of an approved solution to its source, requirement and approver.

The story

An illustrative sceneA twenty-page clinical protocol, drafted with AI help. The first draft contains an explicit rule: do not give a certain drug below a set level of kidney function. By the fifth draft the rule is gone. Nobody deleted it on purpose, and nobody noticed.

The problem first appeared in AI content-generation projects, where explicit constraints got lost between versions. The lab is now taking it into healthcare, where a lost constraint costs far more.

The project is a master's thesis and the first research unit of the HEALTHx framework. Its question is simple: can we show, rather than assume, that every explicit requirement is still in the solution when it is approved?

The idea

Nothing is approved while a requirement is unmet.

  1. Source and explicit requirement
  2. Element of the solution
  3. Link and check in the ledgerour added value
  4. Expert review and sign-off

The opportunity

Every organization that drafts clinical or regulatory documents with AI help needs evidence that its requirements were not lost.

Why now

AI has entered protocol and report writing faster than the tools to check it.

What a partner gains
  • An auditable record for every approved document
  • Evidence to present at review and accreditation
  • Training for your team in the method
Who we are looking for
  • Quality and patient-safety teams that write and review protocols
  • Graduate students interested in traceability and verification
Ways to engage
  • Case study with your quality team
  • Co-supervision of theses
  • Development within HEALTHx

Our five questions

1. Problem
When AI helps draft a clinical protocol or report, it can drop or distort an explicit requirement without anyone noticing.
2. Current solution
Expert review by reading, and document version control.
3. Why it falls short
Review does not systematically tie each element to its source and requirement, so a lost constraint is hard to catch or audit later.
4. Our solution
A ledger that ties each element to its source, requirement, version and sign-off, so nothing is approved while a requirement is unmet.
5. Impact
An auditable trail for every decision, evaluated on five genomic case studies with a clinical genomics advisor.

The journey so far

  1. DoneProblem definition and initial ledger design
  2. NowDesigning experiments on five genomic case studies
  3. NextApprove the experiment scope; defence in spring 2027

What we do not claim

  • Early results came from a non-clinical setting, are not yet peer reviewed, and are not generalized to healthcare.
  • The current evaluation covers five case studies.
  • HEALTHx is currently in research and development. Clinical and commercial outcomes depend on evidence and future partnerships.

Pillar 1
Decision stability: It checks that protecting an image and tracing its origin do not change the diagnostic decision.

The story

An illustrative sceneA patient's brain MRI moves from the hospital to an AI service, then to a research database. Along the way it is converted to another format and its header data is stripped. A year later nobody knows where it came from, or whether it was cleared for use.

The obvious fix is to hide a mark inside the image itself, so it is not lost with the header. But that raises a harder question: does the mark, however small, change the diagnostic model's decision for this particular patient?

Two lines of the lab's work meet here: protecting health data and tracing its origin, and the RIDI method for measuring decision flips. Earlier studies measure image quality and average accuracy; this project measures what happens to each case.

The idea

Marking is diagnostically safe only if the decision does not flip.

  1. Medical image
  2. Reversible provenance mark
  3. Model before and after marking
  4. Case-level decision-flip testour added value

The opportunity

Medical images are increasingly shared for AI and research, yet there is little evidence on when a provenance mark is diagnostically safe.

Why now

Images moving to cloud AI services and shared research projects.

What a partner gains
  • Evidence-based guidance on choosing a safe payload size
  • A way to prove image provenance in your research projects
  • Co-authorship of the research
Who we are looking for
  • Radiology departments and imaging centers interested in image provenance
  • Researchers in medical image processing and data security
Ways to engage
  • Data partnership after approvals
  • Co-supervision
  • Research grant

Our five questions

1. Problem
Medical images move between hospitals, AI services and research, losing their provenance, and protection marks may change a diagnostic model's decision.
2. Current solution
DICOM header metadata and digital signatures, and watermarking studies that measure image quality and average accuracy.
3. Why it falls short
Headers can be stripped when images are converted, and average accuracy hides a decision flip for an individual patient.
4. Our solution
A reversible provenance mark inside the image, with a case-level decision-flip test using the RIDI method at several payload sizes.
5. Impact
Knowing when marking is diagnostically safe and when it is not, starting with a pilot on 200 images from the public BraTS dataset.

The journey so far

  1. NowDeveloping the PhD proposal
  2. NextProposal decision in December 2026
  3. NextPilot on 200 BraTS images

What we do not claim

  • The first stage uses public data, with no patient data.
  • Results apply only to the models and payload sizes tested.

When the scientific evidence changes, the case comes back to the table, safely and on the record.

Problem: Many rare-disease patients remain undiagnosed; new scientific evidence could change their result, but their cases are not revisited systematically.

The opportunity: Every inconclusive genomic case in the archive may be a postponed diagnosis. Governed reanalysis turns the archive into value for patients and the hospital.

Pillar 3
Evidence through change: It reopens a genomic decision, with evidence and a record, whenever the science changes.

The story

An illustrative sceneA child with a rare disease had genomic testing three years ago and the result was "inconclusive". Since then, new evidence has been published about one of the variants in the file. If the case were reanalyzed today, the result might change. But the file sits in an archive nobody revisits.

The project extends the lab's bioinformatics work since the PhD, and a clinical genomics collaboration with experts in the field. International ACMG guidance recommends reanalysis, and open tools such as Talos flag cases automatically.

But a flag is not a diagnosis. Between "this case deserves review" and "we have told the family" there is a review, a consultant's sign-off, and a decision that must be recorded. That is the link this project builds, without replacing existing tools.

The idea

A governed operating layer on top of open reanalysis tools.

  1. New scientific evidence
  2. Automated flagging with open tools
  3. Review, consultant sign-off, decision recordour added value
  4. Recontacting the patient

The opportunity

Every inconclusive genomic case in the archive may be a postponed diagnosis. Governed reanalysis turns the archive into value for patients and the hospital.

Why now

New genomic evidence keeps accumulating, and international guidance recommends reanalysis.

What a partner gains
  • A safe reanalysis pathway that fits your clinic
  • An auditable decision record
  • A role in designing the tool and publishing its results
Who we are looking for
  • Hospitals with genetics clinics and genomics laboratories
  • Researchers in bioinformatics and clinical genomics
Ways to engage
  • Joint feasibility study
  • Trial on de-identified data after approvals
  • Research grant

Our five questions

1. Problem
Many rare-disease patients remain undiagnosed; new scientific evidence could change their result, but their cases are not revisited systematically.
2. Current solution
Periodic reanalysis under ACMG guidance, and open automated tools such as Talos.
3. Why it falls short
The tools flag cases, but they do not govern review, consultant sign-off, recontacting the patient, or the decision record.
4. Our solution
An operating layer on top of open tools that versions evidence changes, requires the consultant's sign-off and keeps an auditable decision record.
5. Impact
New diagnoses reaching patients safely and traceably.

The journey so far

  1. NowFeasibility study and review of existing tools
  2. NextA first software adapter on top of an open tool
  3. NextGo or pivot decision

What we do not claim

  • We are not building a replacement variant-interpretation tool.
  • The clinical decision belongs to the consultant; the system records it and does not make it.
  • No patient data before ethics approval.

Our research school

Five questions every project starts with

We start from the problem, not the tool or the technique. Every project in the lab, and every thesis it supervises, answers these questions in order before the first line of code.

  1. What is the problem?

    Who it affects, and how we know it is real.

  2. How is it solved now?

    The existing solutions in research and practice.

  3. Why is that not good enough?

    The specific gap, shown with evidence.

  4. How will we solve it?

    Our added value, not a repeat of what exists.

  5. What is the impact?

    What changes for those who use the solution, and how we measure it.

Manuscript · submission-ready, not yet peer reviewed · 2026-09-26

Equal evaluation scores do not certify equivalent AI behaviour

In a preregistered retrieval experiment, five standard retrieval metrics were identical, yet benchmark-defined answer correctness changed for 17.27% of 800 queries. Registered extensions, an exact control frontier and a production update test the limits of that result. Submission-ready; not yet peer reviewed, accepted or published.

What a partner can expect

  • Reproducibility with documented code and data, open where the agreement allows.
  • Claims sized to evidence decision stability is not correctness, and an evaluation is never sold as certification.
  • Data protection under Saudi PDPL, and no patient data without ethics approval.
